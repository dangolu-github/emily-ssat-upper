The Frontier
and the Gold Rush

Background for American history passages about moving west, 1803–1890.

Four things to hold on to

  • Expansion came in steps: purchase (1803), trails (1840s), war with Mexico (1846–48), gold (1848), railroad (1869).
  • “Gold Rush” names a crowd, not a steady business: most miners lost; merchants won.
  • Every gain for settlers was a loss for Native peoples; passages often carry both in one paragraph.
  • The “frontier” is a line that moved west until 1890, and then a story Americans told about themselves.

Why people moved west

In 1803 the United States bought the Louisiana Territory from France and doubled its size; Lewis and Clark crossed it in 1804–06. By the 1840s many Americans believed in “Manifest Destiny”: the idea that the nation was meant to stretch to the Pacific. Families travelled in wagon trains along the Oregon Trail; Texas (1845), Oregon (1846) and, after the war with Mexico (1846–48), California and the Southwest became American.

The California Gold Rush, 1848–1855

In January 1848 James Marshall found gold at Sutter’s Mill on the American River. Word spread, and in 1849 about 90,000 people arrived by land and sea: the “forty-niners”. They came from the eastern states, Mexico, Chile, China, Australia and Europe. San Francisco grew from a village of about 1,000 to a city of 25,000 within two years, and California became a state in 1850, far ahead of the usual timetable.

What the rush was really like

Early miners panned loose gold from streams; within a few years the easy gold was gone and mining needed machinery, companies and wage labour. Most miners never got rich. The steady fortunes went to merchants who sold shovels, food and trousers (Levi Strauss), to bankers and to shipping lines. Mining camps had no courts at first, so miners made their own rules and “vigilance committees”; many camps became ghost towns when the gold ran out. The rush was a disaster for California’s Native peoples, whose population fell sharply, and hydraulic mining choked rivers with mud.

After California

Later rushes followed the same pattern: Colorado (1859), the Comstock silver lode in Nevada (1859), the Black Hills (1876) and the Klondike in the Yukon (1896–99). The Homestead Act of 1862 gave 160 acres to any family that farmed the land for five years; the transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869. Plains peoples were pushed onto reservations after a series of wars. In 1890 the Census Bureau declared that there was no longer a frontier line, and in 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued that the frontier had shaped the American character.

How SSAT questions use this topicHide
  • Who benefited?: Gold Rush passages usually contrast the miners who hoped for wealth with the merchants, bankers and companies who actually gained. Watch for the author’s judgment words: lured, fever, delusion, reckless, shrewd.
  • Cause and effect: Why people went (gold, cheap land, Manifest Destiny) is one question; what the rush did to California (statehood, population, Native peoples, rivers) is another. Keep them apart.
  • Tone: Many accounts mix excitement with cost. An author who calls the rush a “stampede” and lists the dead is not celebrating it; one who calls the miners “adventurers” may be. Choose the degree the sentences support.
  • Period terms: claim, placer, lode, vigilance committee, homestead, wagon train: use the Words box rather than guessing from the modern sense.
