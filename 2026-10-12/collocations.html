Misread Collocations · Set 1

Familiar words, different senses: twenty phrases to read correctly.

Each phrase uses a familiar word in a less familiar sense. The everyday meaning would lead you to the wrong answer in a reading or synonym question.

When you read
  1. Read the whole phrase before you decide what a familiar word means: stem the tide and stem from are different verbs.
  2. Check what the word is attached to: the noun it describes, or the object or preposition that follows the verb.
  3. Swap in a short plain meaning and reread; if the sentence stops making sense, the everyday sense is the wrong one.
  4. In tone questions, read the adjective and the noun together: guarded optimism is still optimism, only weaker.

Describing and judging

01. a pedestrian style

Easy to misread as: Something to do with walking, or a style meant for people on foot.

Ordinary and dull; lacking imagination or flair.

A judgment of quality, not of movement; the noun (a pedestrian = a person on foot) keeps its everyday sense.

Although the essay's argument was original, its pedestrian style made even the most surprising evidence sound routine.
Compare: The city closed the bridge to cars so that pedestrians could cross safely during the festival.

02. a singular achievement

Easy to misread as: One single achievement (the grammar sense of singular as opposed to plural).

Remarkable or exceptional; in some contexts, strange or odd.

It says nothing about number. Praise (a singular talent) or puzzlement (a singular habit) depends on the context.

Her translation of the ancient poem was a singular achievement: no earlier scholar had kept both its rhyme and its meaning.
Compare: 'Mouse' is the singular form of 'mice'.

03. a commanding presence

Easy to misread as: Bossy; a person who keeps giving orders.

Impressive and authoritative; naturally drawing attention and respect.

Describes the effect a person has, not orders given; a commanding lead is a dominant one, a commanding view is a wide one from above.

Although she never raised her voice, the conductor's commanding presence silenced the restless orchestra the moment she lifted her baton.
Compare: The captain was commanding the crew to lower the sails before the storm arrived.

04. a ready supply (a ready means)

Easy to misread as: Prepared, as in finished getting ready.

Easily available; at hand when needed.

With things (supply, means, access) it means available; with answers or wit it means quick. It does not imply that anyone made preparations.

The valley's rivers gave early settlers a ready supply of fresh water, so the first farms clustered along their banks.
Compare: After weeks of rehearsal, the cast was finally ready for opening night.

05. a liberal amount

Easy to misread as: Politically liberal, or open-minded.

Generous; plentiful in quantity.

With amounts, helpings, use or spending, liberal is about quantity, not politics or values; a liberal interpretation is a loose, non-strict one.

The museum's founder made liberal use of his own fortune, buying paintings that no other collector would touch.
Compare: The newspaper's liberal editorials often clashed with the governor's policies.

Attitude and degree

06. qualified praise

Easy to misread as: Praise from a qualified, certified expert.

Praise that is limited or given with reservations.

The praise is real but partial, not a rejection; unqualified success means complete success.

The review offered only qualified praise: it admired the film's photography but called the script an afterthought.
Compare: Only a qualified electrician is allowed to rewire the old theater.

07. guarded optimism

Easy to misread as: Optimism protected by guards, or being defended.

Cautious, restrained hope that stops short of full confidence.

Guarded weakens the noun without reversing it: guarded optimism is still optimism, and guarded skepticism is still doubt.

Doctors expressed guarded optimism after the first trial: the patients improved, but the sample was too small to be sure.
Compare: Two soldiers guarded the gate of the fortress through the night.

08. a measured response

Easy to misread as: Something measured with a ruler or numbers.

A careful, deliberate and restrained response that avoids overreacting.

Signals calm control, not weakness or coldness; measured tone and measured words work the same way. After quantities (measured rainfall) the literal sense returns.

Instead of firing back at his critics, the scientist gave a measured response that admitted one error and defended the rest of the study.
Compare: The nurse measured the child's height against the chart on the wall.

09. a pointed remark

Easy to misread as: Sharp-tipped, or simply a remark that makes a point.

A remark clearly aimed at a particular person or issue, usually critical.

The criticism may be indirect but its target is obvious; a pointed question or pointed silence carries the same deliberate edge. A pointed roof is only a shape.

When the manager thanked 'those who actually read the report,' everyone understood the pointed remark was meant for the two latecomers.
Compare: The tower ends in a pointed roof covered with copper.

10. an exacting standard

Easy to misread as: An exact, correct or accurate standard.

A standard that demands great care and effort and is hard to satisfy.

Describes how demanding a standard, task or person is, not whether it is correct; an exacting teacher is hard to please.

The violin maker's exacting standards meant that he destroyed any instrument whose tone fell even slightly short.
Compare: The pilot needed the exact coordinates of the island before she could land.

Verbs and their objects

11. stem the tide (of something)

Easy to misread as: Originate or come from (the stem from sense), or the stem of a plant.

Stop or slow the growth or spread of something.

stem + object (the tide, the flow, losses) = hold back; stem + from = come from. Look for from before deciding.

Volunteers stacked sandbags through the night in an effort to stem the flood before it reached the library's archives.
Compare: Many of the town's traditions stem from the customs of its first settlers.

12. fashion tools (from something)

Easy to misread as: Put on or secure something; or anything to do with clothing style.

Make or shape something by hand, often from simple materials.

As a verb, fashion means make; as a noun it means clothing style or manner (in a simple fashion = in a simple way).

Using only bark and resin, the villagers fashioned canoes light enough for two people to carry.
Compare: Wide-brimmed hats came back into fashion that summer.

13. assume control (assume responsibility)

Easy to misread as: Suppose or guess that someone has control.

Take on a role, duty or power.

assume + role/duty/power/control = take on; assume + that-clause = suppose; assume an air of calm = put on (sometimes falsely).

When the founder fell ill, his daughter assumed control of the company and closed two of its failing factories.
Compare: Most readers assume that the narrator is telling the truth until the final chapter.

14. realize a goal (realize the potential of something)

Easy to misread as: Become aware of or notice a goal.

Make a plan, hope or possibility actually happen; achieve it.

realize + goal/dream/ambition/potential = make real; realize + that-clause or a fact = become aware.

Only after decades of fundraising did the town realize its goal of building a public library.
Compare: Halfway up the mountain, she realized that she had left the map at the campsite.

15. court disaster

Easy to misread as: Something to do with a law court, or with dating.

Act in a way that makes a bad result likely; invite risk.

court + disaster/danger/controversy = risk inviting it; court + voters/allies/favor = try to win them. Neither use involves a law court.

Climbing without checking the weather report is courting disaster, however experienced the team may be.
Compare: The case will be heard in federal court next spring.

16. harbor doubts

Easy to misread as: A port, or giving shelter to ships.

Keep a feeling or thought (doubt, resentment, suspicion) in one's mind, usually privately and for some time.

harbor + feeling = hold inside, often unspoken; harbor + a person = hide or shelter them.

Although he signed the agreement, the minister privately harbored doubts about whether either side would honor it.
Compare: Fishing boats waited in the harbor until the storm passed.

Phrases that reverse expectations

17. betray nervousness

Easy to misread as: Be disloyal to someone; hand someone over to an enemy.

Show or reveal something, especially a feeling, without meaning to.

With a feeling or sign as the object (nervousness, fear, an accent), no one is disloyal; betray a friend or a country keeps the disloyal sense.

The candidate spoke calmly, but her trembling hands betrayed her nervousness.
Compare: The spy betrayed his country by selling its codes to a foreign government.

18. sanction a plan

Easy to misread as: Always punish (from economic sanctions).

As a verb, usually: officially approve or permit. As a plural noun (sanctions): penalties.

One word with two opposite senses: sanction a plan = approve; impose sanctions = punish. Check the grammar and who is acting.

The school board refused to sanction the trip until every family had signed the safety form.
Compare: The council imposed sanctions on the factory for dumping waste into the river.

19. found wanting

Easy to misread as: Wanted or desired by someone.

Judged not good enough; shown to be inadequate when tested.

Here wanting means lacking or deficient; wanting in + a quality means lacking it (wanting in courage).

The bridge design looked elegant on paper, but it was found wanting when engineers tested it against hurricane-force winds.
Compare: After the long hike, the children were wanting nothing more than a cold drink.

20. all but impossible

Easy to misread as: Everything except impossible, so possible.

Almost, very nearly (impossible).

all but + adjective or verb = almost (all but forgotten); all but + noun = every one except (all but one student). Look at the next word.

Without a map or a compass, finding the trail again after dark was all but impossible.
Compare: All but two of the paintings survived the fire.
