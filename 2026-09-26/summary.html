Evidence Words in Context and the Early Republic

Two homework questions revisited, fifteen words retrieved in one connected text, and a first reading on American government.

We began with the two analogy questions from last week's homework, then completed the fifteen-blank text How Accounts Become Evidence, and finished with the background page and passage Government responsibilities in the early republic. The explanations below record the distinctions that mattered in class and add a few consolidation examples.

1. The two analogies, repaired

conjecture is to judgment: a kind of, not a step toward

A conjecture (noun) is a judgment made before the evidence is complete: an assumption, a hypothesis, a guess in formal language. The relationship is therefore a conjecture is a kind of judgment, just as a sonnet is a kind of poem.

The tempting option was evidence is to conclusion. That pair is a sequence: you collect evidence first and reach a conclusion afterwards. A conjecture is not a required first step toward every judgment; we can judge from observation, from other people's reasoning, or from instinct. So the connector leads to does not fit the stem, and is a kind of does.

Two more relationships from the same question: a chapter is part of a novel, and an archive holds manuscripts (a manuscript is simply a written document). Neither is a kind of.

Before the test results came back, the doctor's diagnosis was only a conjecture.

credible is to credulous: check both columns

Credible = believable, worthy of trust; it describes a story, source or explanation. Credulous = gullible, too ready to believe; it describes a person. One word is positive, the other negative. The only option that keeps a positive word beside a negative one is believable is to gullible.

Genuine is to convincing fails because both words are positive. Persuasive is to articulate fails for the same reason: articulate means expressing ideas clearly and fluently, a positive quality. Decisive (making decisions firmly and quickly) and certain are also both positive.

The witness was credible, but the credulous jury would have believed anyone.

2. Retrieval text: How Accounts Become Evidence

All fifteen blanks were completed. These are the distinctions that needed discussion.

corroborate · substantiate · scrutinize · repudiate

WordWhat it doesTypical objectTone
corroborateprovide additional evidence that supports an account already givena claim, a study, an accountpositive (supporting)
substantiategive a claim enough evidence to stand; related to substantial (large, strong)a claim, an argumentpositive (supporting)
scrutinizeexamine closely and thoroughly; a process word, not a result wordconnections, sources, detailsneutral
repudiatereject or withdraw explicitlyan interpretation, a claimnegative (rejecting)

Blank 10 was the hard one: To substantiate a claim, the historian must ______ the connections among sources as well as the information they contain. The object is connections, something you examine, not something you prove. Reading a connection carefully does not guarantee a supporting result, so the sentence needs the neutral process word scrutinize, not corroborate.

A quick test that worked in class: decide first whether the blank needs a supporting word, a rejecting word, or a neutral process word, then choose among the three options.

Two independent diaries corroborate the traveller's account. The receipts substantiate her claim about the cost. Before publishing, the editor scrutinized every footnote.

conclusive · tentative · authentic

Conclusive settles a question; tentative is uncertain, not final, open to revision. They are opposites, and remain in a conclusion should remain tentative points to caution rather than certainty. Every study has limits, because one method cannot cover every aspect, so careful researchers keep their conclusions tentative.

Authentic is about genuineness: real methods, real evidence, nothing faked. It describes a document or a study's methodology and evidence, not the strength of its conclusion.

The fingerprint was conclusive. The dating of the letter is tentative until the ink is tested. The diary is authentic, though its writer may still have been mistaken.

impartial · skeptical · spurious

Impartial = fair, giving favorable and unfavorable evidence the same consideration. Skeptical = hesitant to accept a claim without evidence; a skeptical reader questions a repeated story, while a credulous reader accepts it. Spurious = false, not genuine, fabricated; a spurious letter is a fake letter.

Words met alongside the text: methodology (the set of methods a study uses), questionnaire (a written list of questions given to many people), field study (research done in the real setting), interview.

An impartial referee, a skeptical customer, a spurious signature.

3. American Independence and Government: terms to recognise

Colonies and the founding

A colony is territory controlled by another country; colonization is the process of taking and settling it; colonists or settlers are the people who settle there. The thirteen British colonies declared their separation in the Declaration of Independence (1776). The early republic means the first decades after independence, when the new government was being organised.

Three branches and how they limit each other

BranchBodyJob
legislativeCongressmakes laws; regulates trade and commerce
executivethe President and the government departmentscarries out (enforces) the laws
judicialthe courtsinterprets the laws and decides cases

Each branch limits the others; the term for this is checks and balances, where a check is a limit. A passage will normally explain the limit it is testing rather than assume the term.

An amendment is a formal change or addition to a law or constitution: it modifies or supplements what was there before. The Bill of Rights is the first ten amendments. Civil liberties are the basic freedoms protected from government control, typically speech, religion and privacy; this is not the same as fairness between two sides, which is justice.

Congress passed the law; the President enforced it; the Supreme Court later interpreted it. The Nineteenth Amendment changed who could vote.

Passage: Government responsibilities in the early republic

The passage is a leader's message to the legislature after an epidemic. Context words that identify the situation: malady (illness), pestilence (deadly epidemic disease), ravages (severe damage), assemble in safety at the seat of Government, Legislature of the Union, health laws of the respective States, Congress, who alone can regulate trade. Divine Providence refers to God's care, common in eighteenth-century formal writing. Revered means deeply respected. Spoilage is food going bad.

Question 1 (ravages). First read the whole sentence from But when we reflect to the next full stop, not only lines 6–7. Options with no support anywhere in the passage go first: no hurricane or natural disaster, no battle, no strike. Spoilage of food shares only the word sea ports. The passage keeps naming a disease and its interruption of business, so disease affecting people in business is the answer.

Question 2 (Congress). The anchor sentence is Congress, who alone can regulate trade; the inference is that Congress has power to regulate commerce. Has failed in its duties is an absolute judgment the passage never makes; revered by the people is about emotion in a passage about duties; previously passed health laws confuses in aid of the health laws of the respective States with Congress passing them; revenue is mentioned only as something to protect.

Question 3 (visited). Plug each option into cities and towns have been visited by a pestilence. Joined in and traveled do not fit a disease striking a town; afflicted keeps both the meaning and the harmful tone.
