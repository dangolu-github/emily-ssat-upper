Answer

Review

Give a one-word meaning for credible and a one-word meaning for credulous. Then check that both words of your option match.

Review 2. credible is to credulous as

  1. persuasive is to articulate
  2. believable is to gullible
  3. familiar is to sympathetic
  4. genuine is to convincing
  5. decisive is to certain
Show answerHide answer

Answer

Credible = believable (worthy of belief). Credulous = gullible (too ready to believe). Both matches must hold: believable ↔ credible, gullible ↔ credulous.

D fails on the second word: convincing describes something that makes others believe; it does not describe a person who believes too easily. The report was credible because its claims were independently verified. A credulous reader accepted the rumor without checking it.

How Accounts Become Evidence

Read each sentence and choose the word that best fits its meaning. Each blank offers three words from last week's lesson. Click a blank to reveal its answer; click again to hide it.

A historical account does not become evidence merely by being repeated. Repetition may make a narrative familiar, and familiarity may make it seem (A. tentative / B. plausible / C. spurious); neither process establishes that its details are accurate. To decide whether an account is (A. credible / B. credulous / C. conclusive), a historian must examine how it originated and what independent records can support it. A (A. impartial / B. skeptical / C. credulous) reader accepts the repeated story without asking for evidence; a careful reader makes belief depend on the quality of the support.

One difficulty is that the reliability of a document and the reliability of a statement within it are separate questions. A diary whose paper and handwriting establish its claimed origin is (A. spurious / B. authentic / C. tentative), yet it may describe an event its writer knew only through rumor. Conversely, a (A. spurious / B. credible / C. conclusive) letter—a document falsely attributed to its supposed writer—may refer to a journey whose occurrence is established elsewhere. Discovering that the letter is false can (A. corroborate / B. scrutinize / C. refute) a claim about the document without disproving every event mentioned in it. A historian may choose to (A. substantiate / B. repudiate / C. corroborate) an earlier interpretation, explicitly withdrawing her endorsement of it; she must still identify precisely which part of the interpretation has failed.

Independent agreement can strengthen an account, but agreement is not always independent. Three later biographies may appear to (A. corroborate / B. refute / C. scrutinize) one another while relying on a single unexamined interview. To (A. repudiate / B. scrutinize / C. substantiate) a claim—to give it evidential support—the historian must (A. corroborate / B. scrutinize / C. repudiate) the connections among sources as well as the information they contain. The number of documents is less informative than the relation between those documents and the event under investigation. A detail preserved only through repeated quotation does not acquire three separate witnesses.

Such caution need not lead to universal disbelief. An (A. credulous / B. skeptical / C. impartial) investigator gives favorable and unfavorable evidence the same fair consideration, while remaining (A. skeptical / B. credible / C. authentic) of embellishments that lack support. A dated receipt, for example, may be (A. tentative / B. conclusive / C. credulous) about whether a purchase occurred, settling that narrow question without settling the purchaser’s motive. Where the records permit more than one explanation, a conclusion should remain (A. conclusive / B. authentic / C. tentative); where they supply no adequate basis for choosing among explanations, (A. conjecture / B. conclusive / C. credulous) should be recognized as such. The task is neither to preserve every attractive story nor to discard every imperfect one. It is to give each conclusion the degree of confidence that its particular evidence warrants.
