Reading Poetry:
Speaker, Images and Turns

Who is speaking, what the pictures carry, and where the poem turns.

  1. Who: Who is speaking, and to whom?
  2. Map: Give each stanza a short job (setting, person, feeling, turn).
  3. Images: Mark pictures and comparisons; ask what feeling each one carries.
  4. Answer: Choose the option the lines support, at the poem’s own strength.

Poem 1 · November

There is wind where the rose was,Cold rain where sweet grass was,And clouds like sheepStream o’er the steep5Grey skies where the lark was.

Nought warm where your hand was,Nought gold where your hair was,But phantom, forlorn,Beneath the thorn,10Your ghost where your face was.

Cold wind where your voice was,Tears, tears where my heart was,And ever with me,Child, ever with me,15Silence where hope was.

Word help (not given on the test): o'er = over; the steep = a steep hillside; lark = a small songbird; phantom = a ghost-like figure; forlorn = sad and alone; thorn = a thorny bush.

A Images, comparisons and patterns

Images, comparisons and patterns.

  • Image: a picture built from sense details (cold rain, grey skies). Ask what feeling it carries.
  • Comparison: like or as marks a simile; a metaphor compares without them. Name both sides, then what they share.
  • Pattern: a repeated line shape points to the poem’s main contrast.
  • Old or poetic words (o’er, ’tis): read the whole line, then test a meaning in its place.

Worked example

1. To what does the poet compare the clouds?

  1. the lark
  2. sheep sitting still
  3. running sheep
  4. grey skies
  5. his unhappiness
Show answerHide answer

Worked reasoning

Job: name both sides of a comparison. Evidence: “And clouds like sheep / Stream o’er the steep” (lines 3–4). Like marks a simile: the clouds are compared to sheep. Stream means flow or move quickly, so the sheep are moving.

A. The lark is a different image in line 5. B. Sitting still contradicts stream. C. Matches both parts: sheep, moving. D. Grey skies sit next to the clouds in the stanza, but they are not what the clouds are compared to. E. The image may add to the sad mood, but the clouds are not compared to unhappiness. Answer: C.

Practice

2. What is the most likely meaning of the word “nought” as it appears in lines 6 and 7?

  1. enough
  2. do not
  3. although
  4. even more
  5. nothing
Show answerHide answer

Answer

Answer: E. Put the choice back into the line: “Nothing warm where your hand was, / Nothing gold where your hair was.” The hand and hair are gone, so nothing warm or golden is left. B keeps the idea of “not” but does not fit the grammar (“do not warm where your hand was”). A, C and D make no sense in the line.

B Speaker, listener and the whole poem

Speaker, listener and the whole poem.

  • Speaker: the voice saying I or my. Call it the speaker; it is not automatically the poet.
  • Listener: who you is. A name or title set off by commas (Child, …) is the person being spoken to.
  • Turn: the place where the subject or feeling shifts. The stanza map shows it.
  • Whole-poem meaning: topic + what the poem says about it. It must fit every stanza, not just one.
  • Strength: a strong answer (all, never, forever) is right only when the poem’s own words are that strong.

Worked example

3. Which of the following best summarizes the meaning of the poem?

  1. November has come and the terrible weather dampens the poet’s mood.
  2. November is over and hope will return as soon as sun and warmth replace the seasonal greyness.
  3. The poet has lost someone dear to him, and sees his loss reflected in the season of November.
  4. The poet cannot find his child because it is so dark and windy outside.
  5. The poet longs to escape from the awful season of November.
Show answerHide answer

Worked reasoning

Job: whole-poem meaning, so the answer must fit all three stanzas. Evidence: the stanza map — the season (stanza 1), a person who is gone: “Your ghost where your face was” (stanza 2), and the speaker’s grief (stanza 3).

A. Reverses the cause: the sadness comes from the loss, not from the weather. B. Contradicts the last line, which ends with silence, not returning hope. C. Covers all three stanzas: a lost person, and a season that looks like that loss. D. Takes ghost and the dark weather literally; the person is gone, not hard to see. E. Escape is never mentioned. Answer: C.

Practice

4. In the last stanza, the poet suggests that

  1. his life is sad now, but it will improve eventually
  2. he has lost all hope forever
  3. he would feel hopeless if it weren’t for the presence of his child
  4. if the child stays with him forever, he may learn to love again
  5. he wishes the child would stop crying
Show answerHide answer

Answer

Answer: B. “And ever with me, / Child, ever with me, / Silence where hope was.” Child, is the person being spoken to, the same “your” whose face is now a ghost. What is ever with me is the silence that has taken the place of hope. Ever supports “forever.” C and D read “Child, ever with me” as if the child were still present. A contradicts ever. In E the tears belong to the speaker (“Tears, tears where my heart was”).
