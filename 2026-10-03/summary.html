1. Two homework analogies

Class 02 homework, Question 3 (wolf is to pack) has been re-marked. Your homework page shows the updated result.

Find the connector in this order

  1. Component (part of). A page is part of a website; a chapter is part of a book; a wheel is part of a car.
  2. Tool and function. A pencil is used to write; a knife is used to cut.
  3. Sequence (order). A seed becomes a plant.
  4. A detailed connector that fits only this question, when the general ones leave two options standing.

Write the connector as a sentence, keep its direction, and test every option with the same sentence.

Page is to website as scene is to drama

The tempting option, string is to violin, passes the general connector: a string is part of a violin. When two options pass the general test, the question needs a more detailed connector. A page is a small unit of a website and the place where you enter it: you reach the whole website through one page. A scene is a small unit of a drama in the same way, and the drama reaches the audience scene by scene. Both pairs are also non-physical: you cannot touch a website or a drama. A string is a physical part of a physical instrument.

Detailed connector: a page is the small unit through which you enter a website; a scene is the small unit through which a drama unfolds.

Arbitrator is to mediate as curator is to preserve

The connector is person : the job that person does. An arbitrator's job is to settle a dispute. A curator is not a museum cleaner: a curator looks after a collection, decides what is shown and how, and keeps the objects safe. Of the options, preserve names that job. Display describes what the museum does with the objects, and ancient describes the objects themselves, not the person's job.

2. Reading a science passage by its structure

On a first read, follow the structure rather than every detail. The structure tells you where to look when a question names a detail.

Observed result (background)→Research question (hypothesis)→Method / measurement→Finding→Explanation (mechanism, theory)→Contribution

A passage may skip a step, but it does not swap the order. An explanation always comes after the finding it explains. A hypothesis is an assumption the scientists test.

The Arctic tern passage, step by step

StepWords in the passageWhat they signal
Background“Scientists long marveled at this feat but struggled to understand…”long + but struggled: the old understanding was incomplete
Method“Recent research using miniature tracking devices has revealed…”the new tool that made the difference
Finding 1“…do not fly in a straight line; instead, they follow a winding, S-shaped route”instead: the new result replaces the old idea
Explanation“By riding these winds, the terns expend far less energy…”by + -ing: how it works (the mechanism)
Finding 2“The study also found that terns spend surprisingly little time resting…”a second, parallel finding
Evidence“One of the researchers noted…”a quotation that supports the findings
Contribution“Despite the grueling distance… suggesting that… may actually contribute to their longevity”despite = even though; suggesting and may make it a possibility, not a fact

Four ways a wrong answer fails

TypeWhat happensExample from question R1
Wrong part (mismatch)a detail that is in the passage, attached to the wrong ideabreeding grounds are mentioned in the first sentence, but never as a reason for the route
Outsideinformation the passage never givespredators, stronger muscles, warmer air
Twistedthe passage's own words with the relationship changeda word from the passage used to say something it does not say
Too stronga claim stronger than the passage makesall, always, proves where the passage says may

When you already know where the answer is, match it directly. On a harder question, name the failure type of each wrong option; if you can name it, you can safely eliminate it.

Figurative language

DeviceWhat it doesExample
similecompares two things with like or as“clouds like sheep” (November)
metaphorcompares without like or as; says one thing is anotherthe terns “have essentially mapped the atmosphere”
personificationgives a thing or animal human actions or feelingsthe wind whispered through the trees
hyperboledeliberate exaggerationI've told you a million times.

allusiona brief reference to a well-known person, story or eventhis Achilles' heel (a weak point, from a Greek myth)

symbolan object that stands for an ideaa thorn for pain; silence for loss

In science passages, expect metaphor, personification, and idioms inside quotations. Hyperbole, allusion and symbol are more common in poems and stories. A figurative-language question asks what the phrase means in this passage: answer from the surrounding sentences, not from the dictionary sense of one word.

3. Reading poetry: November

Three kinds of poem you will meet

  • Modern and clear. Usually a scene or a feeling; the words are easy, so the challenge is the hidden theme.
  • Older English (for example, Shakespeare). The difficulty is the language: old words, a changed word order, and shortened forms. Questions rarely test the oldest words themselves; they test the meaning of the line.
  • American poets of the 1800s and 1900s. Many write about social issues: slavery, civil rights, women's rights. A branch or a bird may stand for something in society, so notice the time and the theme.

Centuries: the 1800s are the 19th century; the 1900s are the 20th century.

Changed word order: poets move words to fit the rhythm. Put the sentence back into normal order before you decide what it means: Silence where hope was = Where hope was, there is (now) silence.

How November works

Each part of a poem separated by a space is a stanza. Questions point to stanzas and line numbers, and one sentence can run across several lines.

Every line repeats one pattern: X where Y was. Something cold or empty is now where something warm or living used to be.

StanzaWhat is goneWhat is there nowJob
1the rose, sweet grass, the larkwind, cold rain, grey skiesthe season turns cold
2your hand, your hair, your facenothing warm, nothing gold, your ghosta loved person is gone
3your voice, my heart, hopecold wind, tears, silencethe speaker's grief

Clues that someone has died: phantom (a ghost-like figure), forlorn (sad and alone), ghost, and beneath the thorn: the thorn belongs to the rose of stanza 1, so the person lies under the ground. Nought means nothing: Nought warm where your hand was = nothing warm is left where your hand was. Clouds like sheep / Stream o'er the steep: stream means flow, so the sheep are running, not sitting still.

Silence where hope was means hope used to be there and now there is only silence: hope is gone. Read as “there is hope”, it leads to the trap answers that say things will improve.

A method for symbolic poems

  1. If one reading does not show the theme, go to the whole-poem meaning question first.
  2. Read its options together. Here two options pointed to a lost person, which revealed the hidden theme.
  3. Go back to the lines and check the theme against the last stanza.

SSAT answers are usually moderate, but a poem can be strong. Choose a strong answer, such as he has lost all hope forever, only when the lines are that strong: ever with me … silence where hope was.

4. Five words: effort and endurance

WordMeaningExample
sustain (v.)keep something going over time; supportA four-ounce bird can sustain a journey of thousands of miles.
expend (v.)use up energy, time or money (not the same as expand, to grow larger)Riding the wind, the terns expend far less energy.
grueling (adj.)extremely tiring and demanding; severe, fatiguingDespite the grueling distance, the birds live for over thirty years.
persist (v.)continue doing something despite difficultyShe persisted with the experiment through three failed trials.
longevity (n.)long life; lasting a long timeThe study linked the birds' longevity to a steady food supply.

persist or insist? Persist is about an activity: you keep doing it. Insist is about a position: you keep saying something is true, or you demand that something happen. He persisted with his practice. He insisted that he was right.

Also from the passage: a tern is a seabird (not term).
