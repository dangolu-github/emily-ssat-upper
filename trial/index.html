Stories, Claims
and Evidence

A believable answer still needs the right evidence.

What we worked on

We revisited reading questions from your checked practice paper, with particular attention to the Betsy Ross passage. We examined how answer choices can change the meaning, strength or object of a claim.

  • Map the passage: give each paragraph a short label and notice how the argument develops.
  • Return to the evidence: locate the relevant words before deciding between close choices. Reading the passage once is a starting point; the important step is checking the evidence again.
  • Match the strength: “not established” does not mean “never happened.” Check words such as all, only and never against the text; they are not automatically wrong.
  • Keep the object of a claim: questioning a story about someone is different from rejecting the person’s achievements.
  • Use the passage: background knowledge may help you understand the topic, but the answer must be supported by this text.

How believable is it?

plausible · credible · credulous · authentic · spurious

Focus on the difference between an account that deserves belief and a person who believes too easily. A document may be genuine even when part of its account is inaccurate.
