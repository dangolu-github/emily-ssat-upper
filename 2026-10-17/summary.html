Character and work words: learn them as a group

  • These words keep returning as traps in Synonyms and Analogies.
  • Hard-working (a person): diligent (careful and steady; not intelligent) · industrious (hard-working by habit; nothing to do with industry) · assiduous (stronger: great care and persistence) · toiling (working very hard, often at hard physical work).
  • contribute ≠ work hard: it means to give something to a shared result.
  • calculating (of a person): plans carefully and coolly for their own advantage; can be neutral. cunning, sneaky and scheming are clearly negative; scheming stresses secret plotting.
  • bountiful = large in amount and generous, with a positive, celebrating feeling (a bountiful harvest); abundant and plentiful are neutral.
  • fittingly (transition): in a way that suits the situation. It presents an apt example, not a result: different from therefore.

Main idea and “primarily concerned with”: map the structure first

  • Read the first line of each paragraph and give each part a job before you look at the options.
  • The background paragraph is never the main concern. It prepares the topic; the answer covers the parts that follow.
  • Partial match: an option built from one detail or from the last line only.
  • Too general: true, but it misses the focus of the passage.
  • Changed status: the passage says a possibility; the option turns it into a purpose or a certainty.
  • Names of countries or companies in an option are often a detail trap.
Past: the Space Shuttle program ended→Today: two new directions (the Moon and beyond; private companies)→Future: exciting possibilities
Background: sharks in general→Focus: scientists decide to study thresher sharks→Observation: the tail as a weapon, its speed, the success rate→Conclusion: how they hunt

Inference: the smallest step

  • A correct inference adds as little as possible to what the passage says.
  • Round 1: keep the options that fit the passage’s focus.
  • Round 2: if they fail, go back to the detail lines and test each remaining option.
  • Comparison words tell you about the other side: remarkable for any species means other species do worse.
  • Only, always and other absolute words usually make an option wrong; most needs direct support.

When to guess

  • In the Upper Level test a wrong answer loses a quarter point; a blank loses nothing.
  • If you can rule out two options, choose from the rest.
  • If you cannot rule out any, leave it blank.

2. Analogies: use, function and measurement

The connector sentence

  • ___ is used to ___ (binoculars → observe) · ___ measures ___ (thermometer → temperature).
  • Keep the grammar: a verb in the stem needs a verb in the answer (ballast : stabilize :: brake : slow).
  • Not the function: the material it works on (shovel : soil), the place it is used (microscope : laboratory), what it is made of (lens : glass), its user (compass : sailor), a quality (ruler : long).
  • lubricant : friction looks right but fails: a lubricant reduces friction. The pair names what it acts on, not its action.
  • A twig can be used to catch termites, but a twig is not a real tool and termite is the target, not the action.

3. The Frontier: fifteen words

set out (for the West) = start a journey; in frontier texts, leaving the settled East for the open West.

Setting out

WordMeaningExample
exhilarating (adj.)making one feel very happy, lively and energetic; thrillingAfter six months behind a counter, the first morning on the open road was exhilarating.
emancipation (n.)the act of setting free from control, restriction or slavery; the freedom that resultsLeaving the city felt less like a journey than an emancipation.
vivacious (adj.)lively, spirited and full of energy (of a person or manner); not viciousThe driver’s wife was so vivacious, singing over the rattle of the wheels.
expanse (n.)a wide, open and continuous area of land, sea or sky (not “a large amount”)Beyond the last fence lay an expanse of grass.
arduous (adj.)demanding great effort and enduranceThe crossing was arduous rather than dangerous.

From S1–S2: strenuous = needing great effort (S2 answer) · perilous = full of danger · obscure = not clear, hard to understand (not “to hide”) · effortless = easy (the opposite).

Hard country

WordMeaningExample
desolate (adj.)empty of people and bleak; (of a person) utterly lonely and unhappyNo smoke rose, no fence stood, and no one had passed that way in months.
hardy (adj.)positive: strong, able to endure difficult conditions · hardy settlers; like tough, sturdy, robust, resilientOnly the hardy oxen kept their pace through the early frost.
foolhardy (adj.)negative: recklessly bold, taking risks without thinking (not brave, not hardy)Crossing the swollen river at dusk was not brave but foolhardy.
dearth (n.)a scarcity or lack of somethingThe party found a dearth of grass, and the animals grew thin.
relinquish (v.)give up or let go of, often reluctantlyOne by one the families relinquished the heavy things they had sworn to keep.

From S3–S4: FOOLHARDY → unwise · DESOLATE → deserted (empty of people). arid = dry: a desert is arid, but a desolate place need not be dry. bustling = busy and full of people (the opposite). intolerant has two senses: unwilling to accept other views; in medicine, unable to eat or take something (lactose intolerant).

Naming the mood

WordMeaningExample
apprehensive (adj.)anxious or uneasy about something that may happen; milder than terrified. Not apprehend (understand; arrest).She was not yet afraid of the mountains, only apprehensive.
despondent (adj.)having lost hope; deeply discouragedThe men were no longer merely disappointed but despondent.
exuberant (adj.)overflowing with high spirits and energy; stronger than cheerfulThe children were exuberant at the first sight of the river.
placid (adj.)calm and untroubled; positive, not “emotionless”The river that had roared all night was placid by dawn.
nostalgic (adj.)feeling a sentimental longing for a past time or place, often a little sadThe letter home turned nostalgic by the second page.

The moods of frontier writing often move in this order: exhilarating departure → arduous, desolate country → despondent or apprehensive travellers → nostalgic memory. Naming the mood exactly is the first step of a tone question.

4. Background: the American frontier in brief

  • 1803 · Louisiana Purchase: the United States buys a huge territory west of the Mississippi from France.
  • 1804–1806 · Lewis and Clark cross the West to the Pacific.
  • 1840s · Wagon trains on the Oregon Trail; the idea of “Manifest Destiny”.
  • 1848–1855 · California Gold Rush: gold found at Sutter’s Mill; migrants arrive from all over the world, including China.
  • 1862 · Homestead Act: 160 acres of public land for settlers who live on it and farm it for five years.
  • 1869 · The first transcontinental railroad is completed; thousands of Chinese workers built the Central Pacific line.
  • 1890 · The Census Bureau says the frontier line no longer exists.
  • Expect these words in frontier passages: settler, pioneer, homesteader, wagon train, prairie, plains, ford, pasture, ranch, territory, claim.
