Q12 · Anesthetic : painA · lamp : lightE · muffler : noiseMake the connector specific. Used for is too broad. An anesthetic reduces pain, and a muffler reduces noise. A lamp produces light, which is the opposite direction.

The other options in Q12

  • mnemonic : memory · a mnemonic is a method that helps memory: the wrong direction, and a method rather than a tool.
  • exercise : diet · two methods for one purpose (staying healthy, losing weight). You do not exercise “in order to diet”.
  • muffler · (1) a warm scarf; (2) a device that reduces noise, as on a car exhaust.

Diet and eating words

  • fasting · not eating for a period of time, for health or weight loss, or for a tradition or religion.
  • vegan · vegetarian · pescatarian · types of diet: no animal products at all · no meat · fish but no other meat. In an analogy they are types in one category, not degrees.
  • appetite = the wish to eat · intake = the amount of food, drink or air taken in: a daily intake of grain, a sharp intake of breath.
  • consume · eat or drink; also use up: consume resources, time, money.
  • abstain (from) · choose not to do or have something: abstain from alcohol.
  • excessive (too much) ↔ moderation (neither too much nor too little): eat sweets in moderation.

2. Rhetorical devices in poems

Find it → name it → say its two sides → say what it does in these lines.

DeviceWhat it doesExample
similecompares two things with like, as or thanHe ran like the wind. · O, my luve’s like a red, red rose (Burns)
metaphorsays one thing is another, with no comparison wordHer words were daggers. · My candle burns at both ends (Millay: the candle is her way of life)
extended metaphorone comparison carried through several lines“Hope” is the thing with feathers / That perches in the soul (Dickinson: hope is a bird)
personificationa thing or idea does a human action or has a human feelingThe old house groaned in the storm. · The wind whispered through the trees. · The city never sleeps.
apostrophethe speaker talks directly to someone absent or to something that cannot answer: a person, a dead person, an object, an abstract idea; often begins with O, you or thouO Rome, how great you once were! · O wind, where are you going tonight? · Tyger Tyger, burning bright (Blake)
onomatopoeiaa word that imitates a soundThe bees buzzed and hummed in the clover.
alliterationthe same first consonant sound in nearby wordsSilent, soft and slow, the snow settled.
repetitionthe whole word or phrase comes backRain, rain, and still more rain. · Tyger Tyger
hyperboledeliberate exaggerationI have told you a million times. · then ten times happy me! (Sonnet 37)

Three contrasts to keep apart

  • Personification or apostrophe? Personification needs a human action or feeling (whispered, groaned, never sleeps). Speaking to the wind (O wind, where are you going?) is apostrophe; the word you alone does not make it personification.
  • Alliteration or repetition? Alliteration repeats a first sound in different words (soft, slow, snow). Repetition brings back the whole word (rain, rain).
  • Two devices in one line? Name the clearest one, the one the question points at. My luve’s like a red, red rose is a simile (with a little apostrophe in O). In First Fig the device that carries the whole poem is the metaphor of the candle.

What the SSAT tests

  • Devices appear most often in poems, then in narrative fiction, then in descriptive passages.
  • The device must be visible in the lines. Idioms and dramatic or situational irony are rarely the answer, because they depend on background knowledge.
  • Most poem questions need context: name the device, then decide what it means in these lines.

3. Reading a sonnet: Sonnet 37

What a sonnet is

  • 14 lines, one central idea or emotion, often a personal reflection or a problem.
  • A turn: a change in thought, usually near the end.
  • The last two lines are a couplet: they sum up the poem. A question may ask about “the couplet (lines 13–14)” or about a stanza; it will give the line numbers.
  • Shakespeare’s sonnets: love, beauty, aging, time, mortality; also friendship (often a young man or a patron) and jealousy.
  • Older English: thy = your · thee, thou = you · doth = does.

How Sonnet 37 moves

Lines 1–8: lame and unlucky, the speaker takes comfort in the beloved’s worth, like an old father delighting in his active child→Turn, line 9: “So then I am not lame, poor, nor despised”→Lines 13–14: he wishes the best for the beloved and is “ten times happy”
  • This sonnet is not the usual Shakespeare cliché about beauty fading with time. The speaker has his own limits, yet he finds joy through another person.
  • decrepit = old and worn out · engraft = join firmly to something so that it becomes part of it · store = a rich supply.

Q1 · “abundance”: Answer C · another’s exceptional qualities

  • Mark the words before and after: shadow → substance, abundance, sufficed, glory. The movement is toward the positive.
  • thy abundance belongs to the other person, and it is spiritual, not material.
  • A · his own self-worth: about himself, not the other person.
  • B · physical opulence: opulence = great wealth or luxury shown in costly things. This is material, so it is out.
  • D · another’s desires · E · his own material wealth: negative or material, so both are out.

Devices in Sonnet 37

  • Simile · As a decrepit father takes delight … So I … (lines 1–4): the speaker compares himself to an old father.
  • Metaphor · made lame by fortune’s dearest spite (bad luck “lames” him; not a real injury) · I make my love engrafted to this store · this shadow doth such substance give.
  • Contrast · shadow / substance, lame / delight.
  • Hyperbole · then ten times happy me!
  • If a figure describes one thing in terms of another and gives it no human feature, metaphor is the safe name, especially in a sonnet.

4. Poets worth knowing

  • William Shakespeare · sonnets and plays.
  • William Blake · Songs of Innocence (the gentle, joyful side, seen through a child) and Songs of Experience (the dark side of society). “The Tyger” is from Songs of Experience.
  • William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge · nature and scenery that lead to a reflection on life; their poems are fairly easy to follow. Wordsworth: “I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud”.
  • When the SSAT quotes a famous poem, it usually gives the title and the poet.

5. Analogies of degree

The connector sentence

  • ___ is a stronger (weaker) version of ___ · for verbs: to ___ is to ___ to an extreme degree.
  • Direction first: weaker → stronger (trot → gallop) or stronger → weaker (whirlpool → eddy). The answer keeps the same direction.
  • One scale: both words measure the same thing: speed, force, size, feeling.
  • Not degree: a category (echo : sound: an echo is a kind of sound) · two kinds of movement (prance : strut) · a kind of storm (squall : storm) · opposites (drain : flood).

D1 TROT : GALLOP → E · breeze : gale (a trot is a slower pace than a gallop; a breeze is a weaker wind than a gale).

S5 DESPONDENT → hopeless · S6 APPREHENSIVE → uneasy. Apprehensive is worried and negative; cautious is careful and can be positive.

Scales to learn

ScaleWeaker → strongerWatch out
Windbreeze → gust → gale → hurricanesquall = a sudden, short, violent wind, often bringing rain or snow
Raindrizzle → shower → rain → downpour → storm → thunderstormhail = balls of ice; keep it off the snow scale
Snowflurry → snow → snowstorm → blizzarda flurry is a short, light fall of snow
Circling watereddy → whirlpool → maelstromeddy = a small circular current of water or air · a current is water or air moving in one direction, not in a circle
A horse’s pacewalk → trot → canter → gallopstrut = walk stiffly and proudly; prance = move with high, springing steps

These weather words appear in poems and are common distractors in analogies.
