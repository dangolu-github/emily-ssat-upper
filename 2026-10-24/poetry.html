Rhetorical Devices
in Poems

Find the figure, name it, say its two sides, then say what it does in these lines.

  1. Find: a comparison word (like, as, than); a thing or idea doing a human action; a sound you can hear in the words; a word or line that comes back.
  2. Name: simile, metaphor, personification, apostrophe, imagery, alliteration, onomatopoeia, repetition, hyperbole. One word.
  3. Two sides: what is compared to what; what is made human; which sense the picture uses; what is repeated, and how often.
  4. Effect: what the lines make the reader see or feel, in one sentence. The option must match these lines, not the device in general.

A · The devices, and the three ways the SSAT asks

Four families of devices, and the three ways a question asks about them.

  • Comparison. Simile: X is like Y (like, as, than). Metaphor: X is Y, or Y is used in place of X, with no comparison word. Extended metaphor: the same comparison carried through several lines.
  • Making things human. Personification: a thing, animal or idea acts or feels like a person. Apostrophe: the speaker talks to someone absent, or to something that cannot answer (O Captain!).
  • Sound and picture. Imagery: words that call up a sense: sight, sound, touch, smell, taste. Alliteration: the same first consonant sound in nearby words. Onomatopoeia: a word that sounds like its noise (tinkle, buzz).
  • Repetition and exaggeration. Repetition, called a refrain when a whole line returns: a word, phrase or line comes back. Hyperbole: exaggeration not meant literally (ten thousand, a million times).
  • Three stem forms. Identify: Line 6 contains an example of. Figurative meaning: In line 11, “abundance” most likely refers to. Effect: The comparison in lines 1–4 serves mainly to. The third is the hard one: the right option says what the device does here, not what the device is.
  • Traps. Reading the figure literally; an option that names the right device but gets the two sides wrong; an effect that fits the device in general but not these lines; the wrong person (whose quality, whose feeling).

B · One poem, one SSAT question

Read the sonnet once for sense, then open the devices, then answer the question. The question is a real SSAT practice question; the method is the four steps above.

Sonnet 37: one poem, one SSAT question

Sonnet 37 William Shakespeare

1As a decrepit father takes delight
2To see his active child do deeds of youth,
3So I, made lame by fortune’s dearest spite,
4Take all my comfort of thy worth and truth.
5For whether beauty, birth, or wealth, or wit,
6Or any of these all, or all, or more,
7Entitled in thy parts do crowned sit,
8I make my love engrafted to this store:
9So then I am not lame, poor, nor despised,
10Whilst that this shadow doth such substance give
11That I in thy abundance am sufficed
12And by a part of all thy glory live.
13Look, what is best, that best I wish in thee:
14This wish I have; then ten times happy me!

Q1

The speaker suggests that “abundance” is most likely

  1. Ahis own self-worth
  2. Bphysical opulence
  3. Canother’s exceptional qualities✓ correct answer
  4. Danother’s desires
  5. Ehis own material wealth
Show answerHide answer

Answer Canother’s exceptional qualities

Show explanationHide explanation
  • Locate: line 11, That I in thy abundance am sufficed. Thy = the friend’s. So abundance is something the friend has plenty of.
  • The comparison it sits in: lines 1–4 set the simile. An old father delights in his active child; so I, “lame” with bad luck, take comfort in thy worth and truth. The word belongs to the friend’s side.
  • What the friend has plenty of: lines 5–8 list it: beauty, birth, or wealth, or wit, / Or any of these all, or all, or more, the store his love is grafted to.
  • Figurative, not literal: abundance is not money on its own (wealth is one item of four), and not the speaker’s (he is lame, poor, nor despised only while he borrows the friend’s light).

Each option

  • AHis own self-worth: wrong person. The speaker’s worth comes from the friend; line 10 says the friend’s shadow gives him substance.
  • BPhysical opulence: too narrow. Wealth is one of four gifts in lines 5–6, and the list ends or more.
  • CThe friend’s crowned gifts of lines 5–8: the side of the comparison the word belongs to.
  • DAnother’s desires: the only wish in the poem is the speaker’s (line 13). The friend’s qualities are listed, not his wants.
  • EHis own material wealth: wrong person and wrong sense; the speaker calls himself poor.

A Red, Red Rose

Robert Burns, 1794 · lines 1–4

1O, my luve’s like a red, red rose,
2That’s newly sprung in June:
3O, my luve’s like the melodie
4That’s sweetly play’d in tune.

Words to know: luve love (Scots spelling) · melodie melody · play’d played

Name the device. Say its two sides. Then say the effect in one sentence.

Show answerHide answer

Similetwo similes: love is like a rose, love is like a melody

Show explanationHide explanation
  • Two sides: the speaker’s love and a red rose newly opened in June; the speaker’s love and a melody played sweetly in tune. The comparison word is like, twice.
  • Effect: love is fresh and just begun (newly sprung), vivid (red, red) and in harmony (in tune). The repeated O, my luve’s like makes the lines sing.
  • If the SSAT asks The speaker compares his love to: a fresh rose and a sweet tune. Not to June and not to a musician; those are details inside the two pictures.

First Fig

Edna St. Vincent Millay, 1920 · the whole poem

1My candle burns at both ends;
2It will not last the night;
3But ah, my foes, and oh, my friends—
4It gives a lovely light!

Words to know: foes enemies

Name the device. Say its two sides. Then say the effect in one sentence.

Show answerHide answer

Metaphorthe speaker’s life is a candle burning at both ends

Show explanationHide explanation
  • Two sides: the speaker’s way of living and a candle lit at both ends. There is no like: the candle stands in for the life.
  • Effect: a candle lit at both ends gives twice the light and burns out twice as fast. The speaker knows it (will not last the night) and delights in it anyway (lovely light!).
  • If the SSAT asks The candle in line 1 represents: the speaker’s way of life. The speaker’s attitude toward her way of life is: delighted and unapologetic; she addresses foes and friends alike with ah and oh.
P3

“Hope” is the thing with feathers

Emily Dickinson, 1891 · lines 1–4

1“Hope” is the thing with feathers —
2That perches in the soul —
3And sings the tune without the words —
4And never stops — at all —

Words to know: perches sits, as a bird on a branch

Name the device. Say its two sides. Then say the effect in one sentence.

Show answerHide answer

Extended metaphorhope is a bird that never stops singing

Show explanationHide explanation
  • Two sides: hope and a bird: feathers, perches, sings. The comparison is never introduced with like, and it runs through every line.
  • Effect: hope becomes something small, alive and inside us (in the soul); without the words says it needs no reasons; never stops makes it constant.
  • If the SSAT asks Hope is described as: a bird that keeps singing. The quotation marks around “Hope” suggest: the poet is defining the word, holding it up to look at it.

A3

The Tyger

William Blake, 1794 · lines 1–4

1Tyger Tyger, burning bright
2In the forests of the night,
3What immortal hand or eye
4Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

Words to know: frame shape, make · symmetry balanced form · immortal undying; a god

Find three devices. What feeling do they build toward the tiger?

Show answerHide answer

Apostrophe, metaphor, alliterationthe speaker calls to the tiger; burning bright; burning bright, frame, fearful; a question with no answer

Show explanationHide explanation
  • Apostrophe: the speaker speaks to the tiger (Tyger Tyger, thy), which cannot answer.
  • Metaphor: burning bright: the tiger’s colour and energy as fire, set against the forests of the night, an image of darkness.
  • Alliteration: burning bright, frame … fearful; the repeated Tyger Tyger is a call.
  • Rhetorical question: What immortal hand or eye / Could frame…? No answer is expected; the question itself is awe.
  • Effect: wonder mixed with fear, fearful symmetry: beautiful and terrifying at once, and so is whoever could make it.
  • If the SSAT asks The speaker addresses the tiger directly in order to: express awe at its creation. “Burning bright” is an example of: metaphor.
