Words, Evidence and Inference

Choose the precise meaning. Keep an inference within the evidence.

We completed the vocabulary and synonym sections before Analogies, then worked on Reading A: Keep the strength of the claim. The examples below clarify the distinctions discussed in class.

1. The 15-word reference

plausible

Seeming reasonable or likely to be true.

credible

Believable or worthy of trust.

credulous

Too ready to believe something without enough evidence.

authentic

Genuine; actually what it is claimed to be.

spurious

False or not genuine, despite appearing valid or real.

conjecture

An explanation or judgment formed from incomplete evidence.

substantiate

To support a claim with evidence.

corroborate

To provide additional evidence that supports a statement or account.

conclusive

Strong enough to settle the particular question being examined.

tentative

Not final or certain; open to revision.

skeptical

Doubtful about the truth or value of a claim.

scrutinize

To examine something very closely and carefully.

impartial

Fair; not favoring one side over another.

refute

To show, through reasons or evidence, that a claim is false or mistaken.

repudiate

To reject or disown something explicitly.

2. Distinctions to remember

Believable is not the same as proven

Plausible means reasonable enough to be possible; credible means believable or worthy of trust. A credulous person believes too readily. Authentic concerns genuineness; spurious can mean false or not genuine.

The explanation is plausible, but no one has verified it. The letter is authentic, although its writer may be mistaken.

A genuine document does not automatically contain a true claim.

A conjecture is an idea; evidence supports it

A conjecture is a conclusion or guess without sufficient evidence. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that can be tested. An allegation is an accusation not yet proved; testimony is a witness’s account; recollection is a memory.

The investigator formed a conjecture, then compared the witness’s testimony with the records.

The fact that someone makes a statement does not establish that statement as true.

Substantiate, corroborate, illustrate

Substantiate means support a claim with evidence. Corroborate means provide additional support or confirmation. Illustrate means explain or make clear through an example; an illustration alone may not prove a claim.

Receipts substantiate the expense claim. A second witness corroborates the first account. A diagram illustrates the route.

Substantial means large, considerable or important; substantiate is a verb about evidence. Supporting evidence need not be conclusive.

Conclusive and decisive

Conclusive evidence is strong enough to settle the question at issue. Decisive evidence settles an issue; a decisive person makes decisions firmly and promptly. It does not mean stubborn.

The fingerprint was decisive evidence. The captain was decisive: she chose a route and acted.

Always check what the adjective describes.

Tentative, provisional, incomplete, intermittent

Tentative is not definite or certain. Provisional is arranged for now and may change. Incomplete is unfinished. Intermittent means stopping and starting at intervals.

The finished report offers a tentative conclusion. A provisional schedule is in place. Intermittent rain falls on and off.

An uncertain conclusion can appear in a completed report. Intermittent does not simply mean temporary.

Skeptical and impartial

A skeptical person doubts a claim. A questionable claim gives reason for doubt. An impartial judge avoids favouring either side; impartial does not mean undecided. Scrutinize is a verb; scrutiny is a noun.

The skeptical reader scrutinized the questionable claim. After impartial scrutiny, the judge reached a decision.

Doubt describes the reader’s attitude; fairness describes how the evidence is assessed.

Refute, repudiate and withhold

Refute means show a claim to be wrong with evidence or argument. Repudiate means reject or disown it. Withhold means hold back or refuse to give something.

The measurements refuted the claim. The organisation repudiated the statement. The witness withheld a name.

Rejecting a claim does not by itself disprove it. Withholding information does not establish neutrality.

Read the sense that fits the sentence

Reserved: restrained in expressing feelings, or booked. Attentive: paying attention, or showing care. Tally: count; tally with: match. Lenient: not strict. Inquisitive: curious. Hostile: unfriendly or opposed. Eloquent: fluent and effective in expression.

The reserved speaker gave an eloquent answer. The totals tally with our records. The lenient teacher allowed a late submission.

A reserved person need not be timid. Lenient does not necessarily mean supportive.

3. Reading A · Keep the strength of the claim

Evidence → Inference → Limit

Evidence: The narrator’s present freedom contrasts with the “cares and responsibilities” of city life.

Inference: His former responsibilities make the journey’s freedom especially welcome.

Limit: We cannot invent an employment failure, an earlier permanent belief, or a universal judgment about all work.

Question 1: B. Question 2: D. In both, the answer connects the present pleasure with the earlier routine while keeping the timing and strength of the claim.

“Almost made us feel” is a limit

“Almost” prevents us from treating the feeling as fully reached. “Made us feel” reports a response; it does not establish an objective or settled fact.

The evidence almost convinced the panel that the claim was true.

The sentence does not say that the panel became fully convinced. It also does not prove that the claim was false.

The evidence convinced the panel that the claim was true.

Removing “almost” changes the panel’s response, but being convinced still does not by itself prove the claim. In the passage, deleting “almost” alone would not justify every stronger answer choice.

Do not eliminate a choice merely because it uses a strong word. Ask whether this particular passage supports its degree, scope and timing. “Every year,” “long regarded” and “settled fact” each add a claim that needs evidence.

Use context without importing a story

Stage here means a horse-drawn stagecoach; the driver, horses and later “coach” confirm the sense. Toiling and slaving describes burdensome work figuratively in this context.

“Had spent” looks back from the journey to earlier city life. The narrator’s current pleasure shapes that comparison.

Roughing It is a personal travel narrative with autobiographical and literary elements. Narrative does not automatically mean fiction, and a novel is one kind of long fiction. Identify the speaker and evidence before deciding what the author is doing.

Social criticism means criticism of social practices or institutions. A general description of an author as humorous or critical does not prove that every passage contains a plot twist or the same criticism.
