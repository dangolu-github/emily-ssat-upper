Reading

Inference: How Far Does the Evidence Reach?

An inference connects evidence to a conclusion the passage does not state directly. Make the connection, then check that your conclusion adds no unsupported motive, certainty, or lasting belief.

  1. Evidence: Locate the words that matter and identify whose view they express.
  2. Inference: State the smallest reasonable connection between those details.
  3. Limit: Check the object, time, and strength of the conclusion against all five options.

A. Keep the strength of the claim

A reaction on one occasion need not be a settled belief. Keep qualifiers such as almost, seems, and at first when drawing an inference. Strong wording is acceptable only when the evidence supports it.

Across the Plains

From Roughing It by Mark Twain

Class annotation

short narrative fiction - plot twist - social criticism - humor

By eight o'clock everything was ready, and we were on the other side of the river. We jumped into the stage, the driver cracked his whip, and we bowled away and left "the States" behind us. It was a superb summer morning, and all the landscape was brilliant with sunshine. There was a freshness and breeziness, too, and an exhilarating sense of emancipation from all sorts of cares and responsibilities, that almost made us feel that the years we had spent in the close, hot city, toiling and slaving, had been wasted and thrown away. We were spinning along through Kansas, and in the course of an hour and a half we were fairly abroad on the great Plains. Just here the land was rolling—a grand sweep of regular elevations and depressions as far as the eye could reach—like the stately heave and swell of the ocean's bosom after a storm. And everywhere were cornfields, accenting with squares of deeper green, this limitless expanse of grassy land. But presently this sea upon dry ground was to lose its "rolling" character and stretch away for seven hundred miles as level as a floor!

Worked example

1. Which inference about the narrator’s life before the journey is most strongly supported by the passage?

  1. He had decided to leave the city only after failing to find suitable employment.
  2. His ordinary responsibilities made the freedom of travel especially welcome.
  3. He had long regarded every year spent in the city as completely wasted.
  4. He had preferred outdoor work but had been unable to obtain it in the city.
  5. He had postponed traveling because he expected the journey to be exhausting.
Show answerHide answer

Worked reasoning

Evidence. The narrator connects “cares and responsibilities” and city “toiling and slaving” with an “exhilarating sense of emancipation.”

Inference. The freedom feels especially refreshing because it contrasts with the demands of his former routine.

Limit. The passage does not establish professional failure, an earlier permanent judgment, or a particular reason for delaying travel.

Answer: B.

Practice

2. What can most reasonably be inferred about the narrator’s judgment of the years he spent in the city?

  1. He believes that the prospect of flatter country will restore his appreciation of city life.
  2. He has concluded that future journeys will always be more rewarding than work.
  3. He now treats the lack of pleasure in his earlier employment as a settled fact.
  4. His pleasure in the present experience influences how he evaluates his earlier routine.
  5. He regards work as worthwhile only when it allows a person to spend time outdoors.

Choose one answer. Mark the evidence and explain what the closest alternative adds.

Show answerHide answer

Answer: D. His pleasure in the present experience influences how he evaluates his earlier routine.

His response to the present journey is shaping his retrospective comparison with city life. “Almost made us feel” does not warrant a settled belief, a universal rule about work, or a prediction about all travel.
