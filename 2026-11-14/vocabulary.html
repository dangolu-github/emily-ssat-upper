1

Bridge: What Lives in the Clover Field

Read once for the story, once for the fifteen words.

Every June the clover field behind our school burgeons into a purple carpet, and every June our science class counts what lives there. After three summers of counting, we have learned that nothing in the field stands alone. Even the gray lichen on the fence posts is a symbiotic partnership: a fungus and an alga living as one, each supplying what the other lacks.

The clover is fecund, but its seeds set well only when bumblebees visit, and for this kind of clover the bees are indispensable. The bargain is reciprocal: the bees take nectar, and the flowers have their pollen carried. It is also precarious. The bees nest underground, where their nests are susceptible to field mice, which dig them up for the honey, and a field full of mice is a poor place for bees to raise their progeny.

The mice, in turn, are hunted by barn owls from the old shed. A mild, wet spring is conducive to a large crop of mice, but the owls answer with larger broods of their own, so the numbers rise and fall in recurrent waves. The pressure on the mice is incessant: when the owls rest by day, the hawks and the farm cats take their turn. How thickly the clover blooms in any year is contingent on every one of these links.

Last autumn the shed was torn down. This summer the owls were gone, the mice multiplied, and the clover languished, thin and brown at the edges. Our teacher is propagating a few clover plants in the greenhouse as a reserve, and the class is building a nest box for the old corner of the shed. Our report has a one-word title: Interdependence.
