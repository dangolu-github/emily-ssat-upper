Q19 · News report or biography?

  • News report: one event; a date and a place (arrived in New York on Wednesday); time words such as yesterday or today; quotations used to support one statement; little feeling.
  • Biography: a life followed across many events and years (at this stage of her career…, the next year she…), with more about the person’s character.
  • A quotation does not decide it. Ask what the quotation is there to support.
  • Mark the time and place words in the first lines before you choose. One clear time-and-place clue is often enough.

Q17 · Words from the options

  • overhear · to hear something by accident. eavesdrop · to listen secretly, on purpose, to other people talking. Both end with hearing talk that was not meant for you; the SSAT often pairs them.
  • overbooking · selling more seats or rooms than there are.
  • concession · something given or allowed to settle a problem, for example a free upgrade after overbooking. A concession stand is a stall that sells food at a stadium or cinema.
  • Read the connector in one direction only: the action comes first, what it produces second.

Q14 · Three checks for a describing word and a noun

  1. Tone. Is the first word positive or negative? noxious = bad to breathe or touch: negative, like dishonest. Healthy is positive, so E goes in the first round.
  2. “A kind of …”. Dishonest speaking is a kind of speaking; noxious gas is a kind of gas. A healthy look is not a kind of look.
  3. Concrete or abstract did not help here: every second word is a thing or an activity. Skip a check when it removes nothing.

2. Reading: purpose, details at work, main point

Map first, then answer

  • Read the first sentence of each paragraph (and the last, if it is long) to see the plan before a purpose question.
  • Mona Lisa: its fame today → its history → the new technique. Every part serves the painting, not the painter, so Q1 is A.
  • Q2 · Napoleon’s bedroom → B, the fame of the Mona Lisa. When a famous name is cited, ask why that name and not just a date. A long history is only the surface reason.

Plutarch

  • Plutarch (about A.D. 46–120) was a Greek writer. His Parallel Lives pairs the life of a famous Greek with the life of a famous Roman.
  • A line such as from Plutarch, Life of Timoleon gives the author and then the book: Plutarch wrote the life of Timoleon.
  • He tells small, telling moments instead of listing every battle and date, because he wants the reader to see a person’s character. Passages 5 and 6 show that plan at work.
  • SSAT Upper likes this kind of text: an old translation, Greek and Roman names, and history mixed with ideas.

Five traps on purpose and main-point questions

  1. The literal match. An option that repeats the passage’s words at face value is usually wrong when the sentence carries a further meaning (Q13 C).
  2. The wider scope. Two people become a whole nation or group (Q6 E).
  3. True but minor. A point the passage makes, but not its main point (Q14 C).
  4. Beyond the text. A reasonable guess with no line to support it (Q11 B, Q13 B).
  5. Half right. Check both halves of an option; one wrong half is enough to remove it (Q13 E).

You found Passage 3 the hardest: an opening in which the writer talks about his own method and not about a person’s deeds. Ask “what is the writer doing in this paragraph?” before “what is it about?”

3. Vocabulary: the fifteen population words

Meanings checked in class

  • burgeon · to begin to grow or increase quickly; to flourish. The clover burgeons into a purple carpet. Not “to cover”.
  • symbiotic · describes two different living things in a close relationship that helps both, like bees and flowers. Bio- = life; sym- = together.
  • precarious (adj.) · not safe or secure; likely to fail or fall. A bargain that works but can break at any time is precarious.
  • susceptible (to) · easily harmed or influenced by something; vulnerable, prone to. Nests susceptible to field mice.
  • propagate · to grow new plants from a parent plant, by seeds or cuttings; also, to spread an idea.
  • contingent (adj.) · happening only if certain conditions are met: contingent on every one of these links. As a noun it means a group: a contingent of soldiers.
  • interdependence · inter- = between, each other: things that depend on each other.
  • You already knew: fecund (fertile), indispensable (necessary), reciprocal (both sides give), conducive (helpful to), progeny (descendants), recurrent and incessant.

A familiar word in a second meaning

  • table (v.) · to put off discussing a plan or a motion until later (American use).
  • plug (v., informal) · to talk up a product in public to help it sell.
  • shelve (v.) · to put a plan aside for now.
  • When every option starts with a verb, the first stem word is a verb too. Look for its less common meaning.
