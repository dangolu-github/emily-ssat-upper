Q5: three words that look or feel alike

  • impetus (n.) is a thing: a force or a motive. impetuous (adj.) describes a person who acts on a sudden feeling without thinking first. One names what sets things moving; the other names a character.
  • onus (n.) = a duty or responsibility that someone has to carry (The onus is on the seller to prove the painting is real). It shares only the ending -us with impetus.
  • haste = too much speed in doing something. It is about how a person acts, not about what gets a thing going.
  • Learn the options of a question as well as its answer, including the questions you got right: the same roots come back in later tests.

Q13: say what the verb does to the second word

  • Produces it in someone else: galvanize : action · embolden : courage. Ask who ends up with the action or the courage: the other person.
  • Reduces it: mollify : anger. To mollify someone is to make them less angry: the opposite direction.
  • The act and its content: preach : sermon. A sermon is what is preached; nothing is set off in anyone.
  • Expresses it, strongly: lament : grief. To lament is to express grief aloud, with passion; the grief is the speaker’s own.
  • The act and what it is done to: translate : manuscript.
  • em- / en- at the front of a verb often means to make or to give: embolden, enable, enlarge, empower.
  • One meaning, four words, from the plainest to the hardest: motivate → inspire → spur → galvanize. Learn them as one group.

Q18: castle words, and the relationship each pair carries

  • moat : ditch and rampart : wall: a kind of. A moat is the wide ditch round a castle, usually filled with water; a rampart is the thick wall built round a castle or town to keep attackers out.
  • weapon : lance: also a kind of, but the general word comes first, so the order is reversed. A lance is a knight’s long weapon: a wooden shaft with a pointed steel head.
  • quiver : arrow: a container and what it holds. A quiver is the case that holds arrows; it is not another word for an arrow.
  • sentry : gate: a person and the place where he keeps watch.
  • siege : castle: siege is a noun here. In a siege an army surrounds a castle or town and cuts off its supplies until it gives up. It is a way of attacking a place; it does not mean to invade.
  • Say your own sentence for the first pair before you read the options, and check the order as well as the relationship.

2. Poems: what the questions ask

  • One line or one detail (In line 14, the second speaker most likely means…): the most common question on a poem. Put the line into plain words first, then read the options.
  • Who is speaking (The answers in the poem suggest that the second speaker…): work out which lines belong to which voice before anything else.
  • EXCEPT: four options each have a line behind them; find the line for each. The option with no line is the answer. It takes time, so sum up each stanza in a few words first; then each option is quick to place.
  • What a thing stands for (The “inn” most likely stands for…): in a poem a plain object usually stands for something larger. An option that only repeats the object’s everyday meaning is rarely the answer.
  • Inference is the smallest jump from the lines: what the evidence makes true, and no further. “It could be true” is not enough: an option needs a line.
  • Questions that ask for the name of a device are rare.

3. Up-Hill (Christina Rossetti)

How the poem is built

  • Every pair of lines is a question and its answer, so there are two voices. The question marks show the traveller’s lines; the other line of each pair is the second voice.
  • The last answer of each stanza carries the most weight: From morn to night, my friend · You cannot miss that inn · They will not keep you standing at that door · Yea, beds for all who come.
  • Read as a whole: the road uphill is a life; the day’s journey lasts “the whole long day”; the “slow dark hours” are its end; the inn is the rest that waits there, with “beds for all who come”.
  • Travel-sore and weak means worn out by the road. Being tired is not the same as “a hard day of work”: the poem never mentions work, only labour in line 14.
  • Of labour you shall find the sum: the comfort is what all the labour adds up to. The effort is over, and the rest has been earned by it.

Reading an older poem

  • Turn the old words into plain ones before the options: mankind → people; nigh → near; sought → looked for (the past of seek); to haunt a place → to be there often.
  • Read by the sentence, not by the line: the comma at the end of line 7 runs on into line 8, so the two lines are one statement.
  • Decide the feeling of the lines first (dark or bright, a report or a feeling). It usually removes two or three options at once.
  • Strong words in the options: in a science, biography or report passage, always, never, only, without any doubt usually mark a wrong option. In a poem or a story that rule does not hold: match the strength of the lines. Yes, to the very end is certain, so “without any doubt” is right in Q7.
  • When a poem stays unclear, go by elimination: each wrong option fails on one word (any traveller, concert, delicate, hard work). Every question can be answered without the deepest meaning of the poem.
  • Two kinds of poem: short with plain words and a second meaning underneath (Up-Hill): look at the pattern and at who speaks. Long with hard old words (Hardy): the questions are plainer; hold on to the key words.
