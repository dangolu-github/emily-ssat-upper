One long sentence, taken apart (Q22)

  • A colon needs a full sentence before it. Before the colon: a general idea (He made use, also, of this device:). After it: a list, or the explanation of that idea. Everything after this colon is the device.
  • Find the skeleton: subject + main verb + object: he … employed … persons to play or sing, and jesters.
  • Set aside the modifiers: the opener (Monna Lisa being very beautiful), the time clause (while he was painting her portrait) and the clause between commas (who might make her remain merry). A who-clause between commas adds information about the person just named; the sentence still stands without it.
  • Then read the purpose: in order to take away that melancholy which painters are often wont to give. wont = in the habit of. So other painters’ sitters looked sad; Leonardo’s did not.
  • Decode a sentence this way only when it blocks a question. For the rest of a passage, read on.

The test itself

  • A wrong answer costs a quarter of a point; a blank costs nothing. When you have no idea at all and cannot rule out a single option, leave the question blank.

2. Court and Castle: seventeen words

Pairs the test likes to set side by side

  • herald and courier both carry messages. A herald announces in public, on formal matters (a war, a prince’s arrival); as a verb, to herald = to be a sign that something is coming (Dark clouds heralded the storm). A courier carries letters or packages, fast.
  • abdicate and usurp run in opposite directions: to abdicate is to give up the throne of one’s own will; to usurp is to take it with no right to it.
  • jester = the entertainer kept at a court to make the monarch and the guests laugh, not any clown or magician. valet = in old stories, a man’s own servant (his clothes, his day); today, also the person who parks your car at a hotel.
  • entourage = the whole group around one important person: attendants, guards, officials, friends. One bodyguard is not an entourage. sentry = a guard posted at a gate or on a wall to keep a lookout.
  • homage: to pay homage to someone = to honor them in public; in the Middle Ages, a noble’s public pledge to serve his lord.
  • treason is more than betraying a friend: it is the offense of betraying one’s own country or monarch.
  • realm = a kingdom; also a field (the realm of ideas). sovereign = a monarch; as an adjective, self-governing (a sovereign country). reign = to hold the throne; also the years one holds it.
  • You already knew: herald, courier, monarch, sovereign, heir, noble, reign, realm, decree, abdicate.

3. Reading: inference on immigrant history

Background

  • Between 1880 and 1920 more than twenty million immigrants reached the United States, most of them from Europe. They crossed the Atlantic and landed at East Coast ports such as New York (Ellis Island opened in 1892) and Boston.
  • Many lived first in tenements: crowded buildings divided into small rented rooms. The poorest, most crowded districts were called slums.
  • Mary Antin (1881–1949) came from Polotsk, in the Russian Empire, to Boston in 1894. The Promised Land (1912) is her memoir. Jacob Riis (1849–1914), a reporter who had come from Denmark, wrote How the Other Half Lives (1890) about New York’s tenements.
  • The promised land and exodus both come from the Bible: the land promised to the Israelites, and their departure from Egypt. Immigrants used “the promised land” for America.

Q1 · “the people were interested in us, which was very neighborly”B (your answer)I thought marks a belief. Looking back, the writer lets you see that the women and children at the windows were only looking at the newcomers; she took it for a welcome.
Q3 · “a sort of house of detention … on probation”D (your answer)Turn the picture into a plain claim: detention = being held; probation = a trial period; till they can show a certificate of good citizenship = until they prove themselves.
Q4 · why the “upper half” began to askE (your answer)Put the events in order: the upper half did not care so long as it kept its seat; then the crowding and the upheavals below grew so violent that this was “no longer an easy thing to do”. The cause is what changed just before then.
Q5 · “Nothing is left but to make the best of a bad bargain”A · the tenements cannot be done away with and must be made betterYou tried B, then C, then D. Make the best of a bad bargain = accept a bad situation and improve it as far as you can. B is too positive; C (“nothing at all can be done”) is too strong and denies the best; D is a fact, not the point; E is not in the paragraph.
Q6 · Carroll’s attitude toward previous researchersD (your answer)The words about them are has not gone uncriticized, limited, argues: he disagrees with their conclusions. Condemnation (A) needs words of blame; this is research language.

Two habits for inference questions

  • Absolute options: nothing at all, always, never are rarely right in a passage of history or research, unless the passage’s own words are just as strong.
  • Metaphor lines (a bargain, a house of detention): say the plain claim first, then look for the option with the same claim and the same strength.
  • Attitude words: condemnation = strong blame; appreciation = valuing something, or gratitude; indifference = not caring either way (also: having no preference between two sides).

4. Vocabulary: leaving home, finding work

What the cards do not say

  • impel (v.) and impetus (n.) both name a push. Impel stresses being forced, often against one’s wishes; impetus is the push or motive behind a start, often a hopeful one. galvanize (v.) is a sudden shock into action, usually of a group.
  • Galvanize comes from Luigi Galvani, whose electric shocks made a dead frog’s legs twitch. In chemistry, to galvanize iron is to coat it with zinc.
  • penury (n.) and indigent (adj.) say the same thing, very poor, in two parts of speech.
  • exodus = many people leaving at once; influx = many arriving at once. They are opposites.
  • enclave: a Chinatown in an American city, or a French-speaking district in Canada.
  • xenophobia = xeno- (foreign) + -phobia (fear).
  • grievance is not grief: a grievance is a wrong you want put right. To air a grievance = to complain about it openly.
  • exorbitant rents and a pittance in pay often appear together in stories of newcomers’ lives. menial work is low in status and skill, not necessarily easy. drudgery is the hard, dull work itself, not the tiredness after it.
  • You already knew: influx, polyglot, indigent, pittance.
